Chapter 23: Measuring a Nation’s Income

GDP measures current domestic production. Learn the inclusion rules, classify spending correctly, and separate changes in output from changes in prices.

One flow, two ways to measure it

For the economy as a whole, total expenditure on domestic output equals total income earned from producing that output. A buyer’s payment becomes a seller’s income.

GDP is the market value of all final goods and services produced within a country during a given period. ‘Domestic’ refers to location, not the owner’s nationality.

Market prices allow different products to be added together. Both tangible goods and services count.

GDP is a flow over a period, usually a quarter or a year. Quarterly reports can be seasonally adjusted to remove predictable seasonal patterns.

Accounting identityTotal expenditure on domestic output = Total income from domestic production

These are two views of the same economic activity.

Worked example: Location matters

An American-owned restaurant produces meals in France. Which country records the production in GDP?

  1. The meals are produced inside France.
  2. Ownership does not move that production to the United States.

The restaurant’s production is part of French GDP.

Common mistakes
  • Do not add income and expenditure together: that would count the same production twice.
  • GDP follows the country of production, not the producer’s citizenship.

Source: Chapter23.pptx, slides 3–6; Problem Set Ch23.docx, Q1–4 and Q10

The three inclusion tests: final, current, domestic

Final goods are bought for their end use. Intermediate goods are inputs into another product; their value is already included in the final product’s price.

The same item can be final or intermediate depending on its use. Flour bought by a household for home baking is final; flour bought by a bakery for bread sold to customers is intermediate.

A resale of an existing good does not represent current production. Newly produced parts or a newly supplied dealer service can count separately.

Most unpaid household production and much illegal production are excluded because they are difficult to measure. A financial transfer alone is not production.

Worked example: Used car versus new parts

Someone buys an old car, buys newly made replacement parts, and repairs it without pay.

  1. The old car was produced in an earlier period, so its resale is excluded.
  2. The new parts are current production and count.
  3. The unpaid repair time is household work and is excluded from measured GDP.

Count the newly produced parts, not the old car’s resale value or unpaid repair time.

Common mistakes
  • A sale this year is not automatically production this year.
  • Do not count both an intermediate input and the full final product.
  • ‘Excluded from measured GDP’ does not mean an activity has no value.

Source: Chapter23.pptx, slides 4–5; Problem Set Ch23.docx, Q5–9; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q1–2

Classify spending: C + I + G + NX

Consumption (C): household purchases of goods and services, including durable goods such as refrigerators. A newly built house is the key household exception: it is investment.

Investment (I): new capital equipment, new structures, new housing, and changes in inventories. Buying stocks or bonds is a financial transaction, not GDP investment.

Government purchases (G): government spending on currently produced goods and services, including government employees’ services and public works. Transfer payments are excluded because no current product is exchanged for them.

Net exports (NX): exports minus imports. Exports add domestic production sold abroad. Imports are subtracted because imported goods may already appear in C, I, or G.

If a business buys last year’s unsold equipment from inventory, its equipment investment rises but inventory investment falls. Aggregate investment and current GDP are unchanged by the equipment itself.

Expenditure approachY = C + I + G + NX

Y is GDP / total expenditure on domestic output.

Net exportsNX = Exports − Imports

Net exports can be negative.

Worked example: An imported cement mixer

An Egyptian firm buys a newly produced cement mixer from Slovakia.

  1. Egypt’s investment rises because the firm bought capital equipment.
  2. Egypt’s imports rise, so its net exports fall by the same amount.
  3. The two changes offset in Egyptian GDP. Slovakia records the exported domestic production.

Egypt: I rises, NX falls, GDP is unchanged by the machine itself. Slovakia: exports and GDP rise.

Common mistakes
  • New housing belongs in I, not C.
  • Unsold current output added to inventory counts as investment.
  • Do not subtract depreciation when the question asks for gross domestic product.
  • Import subtraction removes foreign production; it does not mean imports are automatically a loss of domestic output.

First classify each entry. Add the entries that belong to C, I, G, and NX. Leave out transfers and avoid subtracting depreciation for GDP.

A clear four-line setup prevents double counting and makes multiple-choice distractors easier to reject.

Nominal GDP, real GDP, and the GDP deflator

Nominal GDP values each year’s output at that year’s prices, so it changes when prices, quantities, or both change.

Real GDP uses one base year’s prices with each year’s quantities. This removes price changes and lets you compare production over time.

The GDP deflator measures the price level of current domestic production relative to the base year. In the base year, nominal GDP equals real GDP and the deflator equals 100.

Economic growth is the percentage change in real GDP. A rise in nominal GDP alone does not prove that production rose.

Slide check: with 2019 base prices of 30 and 100, 2020 quantities of 1,000 and 200 give real GDP of 50,000. For 2021, nominal GDP is 58,300 and real GDP is 52,000, so the deflator is approximately 112.1.

Nominal GDPNominal GDP in year t = Σ(price in year t × quantity in year t)

Use current prices and current quantities.

Real GDPReal GDP in year t = Σ(base-year price × quantity in year t)

Keep the price column fixed.

GDP deflatorGDP deflator = (Nominal GDP ÷ Real GDP) × 100

A deflator of 120 means the price level is 20% above its base-year level, not 120% above it.

Recover real GDPReal GDP = Nominal GDP ÷ (GDP deflator ÷ 100)

Divide by 1.20 when the deflator is 120.

Real GDP growthGrowth rate = [(Real GDPnew − Real GDPold) ÷ Real GDPold] × 100%

The old value is the denominator.

Worked example: Goat milk and bananas: Year 1 is the base year

Year 1 quantities: 200 milk and 80 bananas; prices: 2.00 and 3.00. Year 2 quantities: 180 and 90; prices: 2.40 and 3.20. Year 3 quantities: 160 and 100; prices: 2.50 and 3.10.

  1. Year 1 nominal and real GDP = 200 × 2.00 + 80 × 3.00 = 640.
  2. Year 2 nominal GDP = 180 × 2.40 + 90 × 3.20 = 720.
  3. Year 2 real GDP = 180 × 2.00 + 90 × 3.00 = 630.
  4. Year 3 nominal GDP = 160 × 2.50 + 100 × 3.10 = 710.
  5. Year 3 real GDP = 160 × 2.00 + 100 × 3.00 = 620.
  6. Year 3 deflator = (710 ÷ 620) × 100 ≈ 114.5.
  7. From Year 1 to Year 2, real growth = [(630 − 640) ÷ 640] × 100 ≈ −1.56%, even though nominal GDP rose.

Nominal GDP: 640, 720, 710. Real GDP: 640, 630, 620. Year 3 deflator: approximately 114.5.

Common mistakes
  • Real GDP fixes prices, not quantities.
  • A larger nominal GDP may be caused entirely by higher prices.
  • The deflator is an index level; inflation is a percentage change between index levels.

GDP and well-being: useful, but incomplete

Real GDP per person is a useful average indicator of material living standards. Higher average production can support more goods and services per person.

GDP does not directly measure leisure, unpaid household or volunteer work, environmental quality, or how income is distributed.

An increase in GDP does not guarantee that every person is better off. Distinguish an average from the experience of every household.

Average outputReal GDP per person = Real GDP ÷ Population

An average, not a statement about equal distribution.

Worked example: More output, uneven benefits

GDP per person rises, but all of the additional income goes to a small group.

  1. Average measured output rises.
  2. GDP does not show how that output or income is distributed.

The average can improve without every household’s living standard improving.

Common mistakes
  • Do not describe GDP as a complete measure of happiness or welfare.
  • Per-person GDP and total GDP answer different questions.

Chapter 24: Measuring the Cost of Living

Build a fixed-basket price index, calculate inflation with the old index in the denominator, and adjust money amounts for changes in purchasing power.

Build the CPI from a fixed basket

The consumer price index tracks the overall cost of goods and services bought by a typical consumer. Its purpose is to follow changes in the cost of living.

The calculation has five steps: fix the basket, collect prices, calculate each year’s basket cost, choose a base year and calculate the index, then calculate inflation.

Keep basket quantities fixed. Only prices change in the basic CPI calculation. The base-year CPI equals 100.

Handwritten slide example: fix the basket at 10 loaves, 5 litres of milk, 4 kg of chicken, and 50 litres of gasoline. The 2025 prices are KWD 0.250, 0.450, 1.500, and 0.105; the 2026 prices are KWD 0.300, 0.500, 1.650, and 0.105. Basket costs are KWD 16.00 and 17.35, giving a 2026 CPI of (17.35 ÷ 16) × 100 = 108.44.

Basket costBasket cost in year t = Σ(fixed basket quantity × price in year t)

The same quantities are priced in each year.

Consumer price indexCPI in year t = (Basket cost in year t ÷ Basket cost in base year) × 100

A CPI of 130 means the basket costs 30% more than in the base year.

Worked example: The slides’ lamb-and-chicken basket

The basket contains 10 lb of lamb and 20 lb of chicken. The 2019 base-year basket costs 120. In 2020 both prices are 5 per lb. In 2021 lamb costs 9 and chicken costs 6.

  1. 2020 basket cost = 10 × 5 + 20 × 5 = 150.
  2. 2020 CPI = (150 ÷ 120) × 100 = 125.
  3. 2021 basket cost = 10 × 9 + 20 × 6 = 210.
  4. 2021 CPI = (210 ÷ 120) × 100 = 175.

CPI: 2019 = 100; 2020 = 125; 2021 = 175.

Common mistakes
  • Do not update the quantities when calculating a basic fixed-basket CPI.
  • Do not confuse a basket’s monetary cost with its index number.

Source: Chapter24.pptx, slides 3–9, including handwritten slides 4–5; Problem Set Ch24.docx, Q1–6; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q10 and Q12–13

Inflation is a percentage change, not an index-point change

Inflation is the percentage increase in a price index from the preceding period. Divide by the preceding period’s index, not the new index.

The same increase in index points can imply different inflation rates. An increase from 80 to 100 is 25%; from 100 to 120 it is 20%; from 150 to 170 it is about 13.33%.

To work backwards from a new index and an inflation rate, divide by one plus the inflation rate expressed as a decimal.

In the handwritten basket example, the 2025 base-year CPI is 100 and the 2026 CPI is 108.44. Inflation is therefore approximately 8.44%.

Inflation rateInflation = [(CPInew − CPIold) ÷ CPIold] × 100%

Use the old CPI in the denominator.

Recover the earlier indexCPIold = CPInew ÷ (1 + inflation rate as a decimal)

24% becomes 0.24.

Worked example: 125 to 175 is not 50% inflation

The basket example gives a CPI of 125 in 2020 and 175 in 2021. Find the inflation rate.

  1. The index rises by 175 − 125 = 50 points.
  2. Divide the change by the old index: 50 ÷ 125 = 0.40.
  3. Convert to a percentage: 0.40 × 100 = 40%.

2020–2021 inflation = 40%.

Common mistakes
  • 175 − 125 = 50 index points, not a 50% inflation rate.
  • Use consistent periods: the base-year comparison and the one-year inflation rate are different questions.

Source: Chapter24.pptx, slides 5–6 and 9; Problem Set Ch24.docx, Q5–8; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q11 and Q16

CPI versus the GDP deflator

CPI asks what happens to the price of the consumer’s basket, including imported consumer goods.

The GDP deflator covers goods and services produced domestically, including domestically produced capital goods. It excludes imported production.

CPI uses a fixed basket in the basic calculation. The GDP deflator reflects the composition of current production.

For price-change questions, check two things: Is it a consumer purchase? Is it produced domestically?

Worked example: Three price changes

Compare a domestic café drink, a domestically made industrial tractor, and imported consumer jeans.

  1. Domestic café drink: consumer good and domestic production → both CPI and GDP deflator.
  2. Domestic industrial tractor: capital good and domestic production → GDP deflator only.
  3. Imported consumer jeans: consumer basket but foreign production → CPI only.

Domestic consumer good: both. Domestic capital good: deflator only. Imported consumer good: CPI only.

Common mistakes
  • ‘Capital goods’ are not automatically excluded from the GDP deflator; domestic capital goods are included.
  • ‘Imports’ enter CPI only when relevant to consumers’ purchases; the claim is not that every imported item is in the consumer basket.

Source: Chapter24.pptx, slides 13–15; Problem Set Ch24.docx, Q2 and Q15; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q17

Why CPI can overstate increases in living costs

Substitution bias: consumers switch toward products that become relatively cheaper, but a fixed basket does not fully reflect that response.

New goods: new products increase choice and can make a given amount of money more valuable. A fixed basket misses some of this benefit.

Unmeasured quality change: a better product can deliver more value for the same price. Statistical adjustments are imperfect because quality is difficult to measure.

The chapter’s three problems tend to make measured CPI inflation overstate the rise in the cost of maintaining a given standard of living.

Worked example: Rice becomes more expensive

Consumers can substitute potatoes for rice. Rice prices rise, so consumers buy less rice and more potatoes.

  1. The fixed basket still contains its original rice quantity.
  2. It captures the rice price rise without fully capturing consumers’ cheaper substitution.

The fixed-basket measure overstates the cost increase compared with consumers’ ability to substitute.

Common mistakes
  • Substitution bias does not mean CPI ignores prices; it means the fixed quantities miss part of the response to relative prices.
  • An unchanged price for an improved product can still represent better value.

Source: Chapter24.pptx, slides 10–12; Problem Set Ch24.docx, Q11–14; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q14

Compare money across years and understand indexation

Money amounts from different years cannot be compared fairly without accounting for price levels. Convert both amounts into the purchasing power of the same year.

Multiply the earlier amount by the target-year CPI divided by the earlier-year CPI. This adjusts the monetary amount, not the underlying quantity of goods.

Indexation means an amount is automatically adjusted for inflation by law or contract, such as an indexed wage or benefit.

Handwritten slide check: income of 50,000 when CPI is 80 requires 50,000 × (120 ÷ 80) = 75,000 when CPI is 120 to buy the same amount of goods and services.

Convert a money amountAmount in target-year money = Earlier amount × (CPItarget ÷ CPIearlier)

Both CPIs must use the same index series and base.

Worked example: Which salary has the greatest purchasing power?

Annual salaries are 12,000 in 1970, 24,000 in 1980, and 36,000 in 1990. The corresponding CPIs are 40, 60, and 100.

  1. Express all salaries in 1990 purchasing power.
  2. 1970: 12,000 × (100 ÷ 40) = 30,000.
  3. 1980: 24,000 × (100 ÷ 60) = 40,000.
  4. 1990: 36,000 × (100 ÷ 100) = 36,000.

Purchasing power is highest in 1980 and lowest in 1970.

Common mistakes
  • The largest nominal salary need not be the largest real salary.
  • To move an old amount into a higher-price target year, the conversion factor should generally exceed 1.

Source: Chapter24.pptx, slides 16–18, including handwritten slide 17; Problem Set Ch24.docx, Q16–19; Review Chapters 23-24-25.docx, Q18–19

Nominal interest counts money; real interest counts purchasing power

The nominal interest rate describes growth in the number of monetary units in an account. The real interest rate describes growth in what that money can buy.

For this course’s calculations, subtract inflation from nominal interest. This is the standard approximation used in the slides and problem sets.

If inflation exceeds nominal interest, the real interest rate is negative: the balance can rise in money terms while purchasing power falls.

Handwritten slide example: a KWD 10,000 deposit at 4% nominal interest becomes KWD 10,400. With 2% inflation, the course’s real rate is approximately 2%; with 7% inflation, it is approximately −3%.

Course real-interest calculationReal interest rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Inflation rate

Use percentage points consistently. The course writes this as r = i − π.

RearrangeInflation rate ≈ Nominal interest rate − Real interest rate

Useful when the question supplies nominal and real rates.

Worked example: Find inflation first, then real interest

CPI rises from 225 to 234. The nominal interest rate is 6.5%.

  1. Inflation = [(234 − 225) ÷ 225] × 100 = 4%.
  2. Real interest ≈ 6.5% − 4% = 2.5%.

Inflation is 4%; the course’s real-interest answer is 2.5%.

Common mistakes
  • Do not subtract CPI index levels from an interest rate. Convert the index change into an inflation rate first.
  • Positive nominal interest does not guarantee positive real interest.
  • Keep the course approximation distinct from an exact compounded purchasing-power calculation.

Chapter 25: Production and Growth

Productivity explains much of the difference in long-run living standards. Learn to measure output per worker or labor-hour, distinguish the four productivity determinants, and explain why capital accumulation raises income but faces diminishing returns.

1. Living standards and productivity

Real GDP per person describes the average level of economic prosperity. Its growth rate describes how quickly that average is improving. Neither the total size of an economy nor output growth alone tells you the average living standard.

Productivity is output per unit of labor. Read the denominator carefully: output per worker and output per labor-hour are different measures. Total labor-hours equal workers multiplied by hours worked per worker.

Workers who can produce more goods and services per hour support higher real incomes over time. Output can fall while productivity rises if labor input falls by an even larger percentage.

Class-example figures from slides 3–5: for the US, $22.57 trillion = $22,570,000 million, so $22,570,000 million / 340.1 million people ≈ $66,362.83 per person. For India, $3.48 trillion = $3,480 billion, so $3,480 billion / 1.45 billion people = $2,400 per person. These are figures used in the class examples, not newly verified current country statistics.

Real GDP per personReal GDP per person = real GDP / population

Convert GDP and population to compatible units before dividing.

Output per workerProductivity = real output / number of workers

Use this when labor is measured as workers.

Output per labor-hourProductivity = real output / total labor-hours

Total labor-hours = workers × hours per worker.

Percentage changeGrowth rate = [(new value − old value) / old value] × 100%

Compute productivity first, then its growth rate.

Worked example: Less total output, higher productivity

A firm initially produces 30,000 units using 600 labor-hours. Later it produces 28,800 units using 480 labor-hours. What happens to productivity?

  1. Initial productivity = 30,000 / 600 = 50 units per labor-hour.
  2. Later productivity = 28,800 / 480 = 60 units per labor-hour.
  3. Productivity growth = (60 − 50) / 50 × 100 = 20%.

Productivity rises by 20%, even though total output falls by 4%.

Common mistakes
  • Do not divide by population when the question asks for productivity per labor-hour.
  • Do not treat total GDP as GDP per person.
  • A fall in total production does not necessarily mean a fall in productivity.

Source: Chapter25.pptx, slides 3–5, 7 and 23; Problem Set Ch25.docx, questions 1–5. Productivity numerical example is new practice.

2. The four productivity determinants

Physical capital is the equipment and structures used in production: machines, factory buildings and business computers. These are produced inputs that help workers produce more.

Human capital is the knowledge and skills embodied in people through education, training and experience. Training belongs here, rather than under physical capital.

Natural resources are inputs supplied by nature, such as land and mineral deposits. Technological knowledge is society's understanding of useful production methods. Knowing a better method differs from an individual worker learning to apply it.

Compare inputs per worker when explaining productivity. An economy can add machines and workers together without increasing the machines available to each worker.

Determinants per workerK/L, H/L, N/L and A

K = physical capital; H = human capital; N = natural resources; L = labor; A = technology.

Worked example: Classify the input

A bakery buys a commercial oven, trains its bakers and adopts a newly discovered production technique. Which determinants improve?

  1. The oven is physical capital.
  2. The bakers' acquired skills are human capital.
  3. The new production method is technological knowledge.

Physical capital, human capital and technology can each raise output per worker.

Common mistakes
  • Human capital is not the number of workers or hours worked.
  • A machine is physical capital; learning to use it is human capital.
  • Natural resources are helpful inputs, but the course does not treat them as the only source of prosperity.

Source: Chapter25.pptx, slides 7–9 and 20–23; Problem Set Ch25.docx, questions 6–10.

3. Production functions: scale versus diminishing returns

A production function describes how labor, physical capital, human capital and natural resources combine with technology to produce output.

Constant returns to scale means that multiplying all inputs by the same factor multiplies output by that factor. Doubling all inputs doubles output, holding technology fixed.

Diminishing returns to capital asks a different question: increase physical capital while holding the other inputs fixed. Output rises, but each additional unit of capital adds less output than the previous unit.

On a graph with capital per worker on the horizontal axis and output per worker on the vertical axis, diminishing returns produce an upward-sloping curve that becomes flatter. Constant returns to scale and diminishing returns to one input can both hold.

Aggregate productionY = A F(L, K, H, N)

A higher A allows more output from the same inputs.

Constant returns to scalexY = A F(xL, xK, xH, xN)

All inputs change together; technology is fixed.

Output per workerY/L = A F(1, K/L, H/L, N/L)

This form follows from constant returns to scale.

Worked example: Two claims that can both be true

A workshop doubles its workers and all other inputs. Separately, it adds a second machine while keeping its workers and other inputs fixed.

  1. With constant returns to scale, doubling every input doubles output.
  2. Adding only a machine raises capital per worker.
  3. With diminishing returns to capital, the second machine adds output, but less than the first comparable machine did.

Scaling every input is different from increasing only capital.

Common mistakes
  • Do not interpret constant returns to scale as constant returns to physical capital alone.
  • Diminishing returns means a smaller additional gain, not necessarily falling total output.
  • A flatter production curve does not mean extra capital produces no output.

Source: Chapter25.pptx, slides 8–9, 14–15 and 24; Problem Set Ch25.docx, questions 12–15 and 22.

4. Saving, capital accumulation and the catch-up effect

Saving allows resources to be used for investment in new productive capital. The tradeoff is less consumption now in exchange for more production and consumption possibilities later.

A higher saving rate can increase capital per worker and raise the level of productivity and income. Because capital has diminishing returns, this capital-deepening process does not permanently raise the growth rate in the course's model.

The catch-up effect describes why a capital-poor country can grow faster than a capital-rich country when other conditions are comparable: extra capital has a larger payoff where capital per worker is initially scarce.

Catch-up is a possibility, not a guarantee that every poor country grows quickly. Institutions, education and other productivity determinants also matter.

Worked example: Level versus growth rate

A policy permanently raises the saving rate. What should the long-run claim be?

  1. Resources shift from present consumption toward investment.
  2. Capital per worker rises and productivity increases during the transition.
  3. As capital accumulates, diminishing returns reduce the extra gain from further accumulation.

A higher long-run income level, rather than a permanently higher growth rate caused by saving alone.

Common mistakes
  • Higher saving does not mean higher consumption immediately.
  • Distinguish a higher level of income from a permanently higher growth rate.
  • Poorer countries do not catch up automatically regardless of policy and institutions.

Source: Chapter25.pptx, slides 14, 22 and 24; Problem Set Ch25.docx, questions 11–15.

5. Policies that strengthen productivity

Saving incentives and investment from abroad can increase the capital available to domestic workers. Foreign direct investment is foreign-owned and foreign-operated capital; foreign portfolio investment uses foreign financing with domestic operation.

Education and training increase human capital, while health and nutrition help workers use their abilities effectively. Education has an opportunity cost because time spent studying is time not used for other activities.

Secure property rights, reliable courts and political stability encourage people to invest because they are more likely to retain the benefits. Corruption and fraud weaken that incentive.

Outward-oriented policies support trade and international integration. Research and development, including incentives such as patents, can improve technology, which drives sustained long-run advances in living standards.

Worked example: Match the policy to the channel

A government funds vocational training, protects investors' property rights and supports research. How can these measures raise output per worker?

  1. Vocational training raises human capital per worker.
  2. Property-rights protection strengthens incentives to accumulate productive capital.
  3. Research can improve technological knowledge.

Identify the productivity determinant or investment incentive affected; naming a policy alone is not the explanation.

Common mistakes
  • Foreign direct and portfolio investment differ in ownership and operation, not simply in whether money crosses borders.
  • Import restrictions are inward-oriented policies.
  • Do not ignore the opportunity cost of education.

Source: Chapter25.pptx, slides 13 and 16–18, 21–24; Problem Set Ch25.docx, questions 16–17 and 19–21.

6. Population growth: competing effects

Faster population growth can stretch natural resources and dilute capital if the capital stock does not rise quickly enough to maintain capital per worker. More total workers alone does not ensure more output per worker.

A rapidly growing population can also place pressure on the education system, making it harder to maintain educational resources per person.

There is a potential positive channel too: a larger population can include more scientists, engineers and inventors, contributing to technological progress. For an exam explanation, name the channel rather than asserting that population growth is always good or always bad.

Capital dilutionIf L rises faster than K, K/L falls

The relevant comparison is capital relative to labor.

Worked example: More machines, less capital per worker

Capital rises from 200 to 220 units while the number of workers rises from 100 to 125. What happens to capital per worker?

  1. Initial K/L = 200/100 = 2.
  2. New K/L = 220/125 = 1.76.
  3. Capital grows by 10%, but labor grows by 25%.

Capital per worker falls despite an increase in the total capital stock.

Common mistakes
  • Total capital growth is not sufficient to establish growth in capital per worker.
  • Do not omit the possible innovation channel.
  • GDP per worker and total GDP need not move together.

Chapter 26: Saving, Investment and the Financial System

The financial system connects saving with investment. Master the saving identities first, then explain loanable-funds diagrams by identifying the curve that shifts and the resulting changes in the interest rate and investment.

1. Markets, intermediaries, stocks and bonds

The financial system channels funds from savers toward borrowers who finance investment. Financial markets connect them directly; financial intermediaries stand between them.

A bond is debt: its buyer lends to the issuer and becomes a creditor. A stock represents partial ownership, so selling stock is equity finance. Stockholders' returns depend on the firm's success.

Banks accept deposits and make loans. Mutual funds sell shares and use the proceeds to buy portfolios of assets, making diversification accessible to small savers.

For bonds, compare term, credit risk and tax treatment. Other things equal, greater default risk or a longer term generally requires a higher interest rate; tax-exempt interest can allow a lower quoted rate. These comparisons require holding other characteristics constant.

Worked example: How does the firm obtain the funds?

A company can issue a bond, issue new stock or borrow from a bank. Classify each arrangement.

  1. Bond issue: debt finance through a financial market.
  2. Stock issue: equity finance through a financial market.
  3. Bank loan: borrowing through a financial intermediary.

Bonds create creditors; shares create owners; a bank intermediates between depositors and borrowers.

Common mistakes
  • A bond is not an ownership share.
  • A mutual fund is an intermediary, not a single corporate bond.
  • Diversification spreads holdings; it does not promise zero risk or a guaranteed return.

Source: Chapter26.pptx, slides 3 and 5–6; Problem Set Ch26.docx, questions 1–10; Review Chapters 26-29-30.docx, questions 1–3 only.

2. Saving versus macroeconomic investment

Saving is income not used for taxes and current consumption. Households may place their saving in a bank, stocks, bonds or mutual funds.

In the national accounts, investment means spending on newly produced capital, such as equipment, factories and newly built houses. Buying an existing stock or bond is a financial transaction, rather than directly adding to GDP investment.

A household buying a newly built home counts as investment in this framework. Do not classify every household purchase as consumption or every everyday use of the word 'investment' as national-accounts investment.

Worked example: Classify three purchases

A saver buys shares, a business buys a newly produced machine, and a household pays to build a new house.

  1. The share purchase is a way of holding financial saving.
  2. The new business machine is investment.
  3. Construction of the new house is also investment.

The new machine and new house add to national-accounts investment; the financial share purchase does not directly do so.

Common mistakes
  • Purchasing stock is not the same accounting category as building a factory.
  • A newly built house belongs under investment.
  • Saving and investment can be carried out by different people even when their economy-wide totals are equal.

Source: Chapter26.pptx, slides 3, 6 and 14–16.

3. The saving identities

Begin with the expenditure identity. A closed economy has no international trade, so net exports equal zero. Rearranging gives national saving equal to investment.

Private saving subtracts taxes and consumption from income. Public saving subtracts government purchases from tax revenue. Adding them cancels taxes and gives national saving.

A budget deficit makes public saving negative. National saving can still be positive if private saving is large enough. Keep all quantities in the same units before adding them.

The identity S = I used here requires the closed-economy assumption. For an open economy, do not simply drop net exports from the accounting identity.

Expenditure identityY = C + I + G + NX

For a closed economy, NX = 0.

Private savingS_private = Y − T − C

Income left after taxes and consumption.

Public savingS_public = T − G

Negative when the government runs a deficit.

National savingS = S_private + S_public = Y − C − G

Taxes cancel when the two saving components are added.

Closed-economy investmentI = S

Use only with the closed-economy assumption.

Budget balanceSurplus = T − G; deficit = G − T

A positive deficit amount corresponds to negative public saving.

Worked example: One table, all five answers

A closed economy has Y = 2,000, C = 1,300, T = 400 and G = 500, all in billions. Find private saving, public saving, national saving, investment and the deficit.

  1. Private saving = 2,000 − 400 − 1,300 = 300.
  2. Public saving = 400 − 500 = −100.
  3. National saving = 300 + (−100) = 200; check: 2,000 − 1,300 − 500 = 200.
  4. Investment = national saving = 200.
  5. Deficit = 500 − 400 = 100.

Private saving 300; public saving −100; national saving and investment 200 each; deficit 100, all in billions.

Common mistakes
  • National saving subtracts government purchases, not taxes: Y − C − G.
  • A deficit of 100 means public saving is −100, not +100.
  • A deficit does not imply private saving is negative.
  • Taxes are not a separate component of GDP expenditure.

Source: Chapter26.pptx, slides 7–11 and 27; Problem Set Ch26.docx, questions 11–19; Review Chapters 26-29-30.docx, questions 4–11 only. Numerical example is new practice.

4. Tax changes: follow consumption too

If income, consumption and government purchases are fixed, raising taxes reduces private saving and increases public saving by equal amounts. National saving stays unchanged because taxes cancel from Y − C − G.

For a tax cut with income and government purchases fixed, public saving falls by the amount of the cut. What happens to national saving depends on how much of the tax cut households spend.

If households save the entire tax cut, the increase in private saving offsets the fall in public saving. If households spend part of the cut, national saving falls by that increase in consumption. In a closed economy, investment falls by the same amount.

National-saving changeΔS = −ΔC when Y and G are fixed

A tax change alone is not enough information unless household consumption behavior is specified.

Worked example: A tax cut is partly spent

Taxes fall by 80 billion. Households spend 60 billion and save 20 billion of the cut. Income and government purchases stay fixed.

  1. Public saving falls by 80 billion because T falls.
  2. Private saving rises by 20 billion: the 80-billion tax reduction is partly offset by 60 billion more consumption.
  3. National saving changes by +20 − 80 = −60 billion.
  4. Closed-economy investment also falls by 60 billion.

Public saving −80; private saving +20; national saving and investment −60 billion each.

Common mistakes
  • Do not automatically say a tax cut leaves national saving unchanged.
  • Distinguish the amount of the tax cut from the amount households spend.
  • State which variables are held fixed.

Source: Chapter26.pptx, slides 12–13; Problem Set Ch26.docx, question 16. Numerical example is new practice.

5. Reading the loanable-funds market

Saving supplies loanable funds; borrowing for investment creates demand. Put the real interest rate on the vertical axis and the quantity of loanable funds on the horizontal axis.

A higher interest rate encourages a larger quantity supplied and reduces the quantity demanded. Equilibrium is where the two quantities match. At a rate below equilibrium there is a shortage; at a rate above equilibrium there is a surplus.

A change in the interest rate itself produces movement along a curve. A change in saving incentives, investment incentives or public saving shifts a curve. Always identify the cause before naming the diagram movement.

EquilibriumQuantity supplied = quantity demanded; S = I

This chapter uses a single loanable-funds market for the closed economy.

Worked example: Find the interest-rate pressure

At the current interest rate, desired investment is 500 billion but saving supplied is only 400 billion.

  1. Quantity demanded exceeds quantity supplied by 100 billion.
  2. This is a shortage of loanable funds.
  3. The interest rate tends to rise, encouraging saving and discouraging some investment until the market clears.

A shortage creates upward pressure on the interest rate.

Common mistakes
  • The supply of funds comes from saving, not investment.
  • An interest-rate increase changes quantity supplied along the curve; it does not by itself shift supply.
  • A shortage is excess demand, not excess supply.

Source: Chapter26.pptx, slides 15–19 and 28; Problem Set Ch26.docx, questions 20–22. Numerical example is new practice.

6. Policy shifts and crowding out

A saving incentive shifts the supply curve right. The equilibrium interest rate falls and the quantity of loanable funds and investment rises, holding the demand curve fixed.

An investment incentive, such as an investment tax credit, shifts demand right. The equilibrium interest rate and quantity of funds rise, holding supply fixed.

A larger government deficit reduces public saving and national saving, shifting supply left in the course's model. The interest rate rises and private investment falls. This reduction in investment is crowding out.

A larger budget surplus has the opposite effect: supply shifts right, the interest rate falls and investment rises. Reduced capital formation can also weaken future productivity and output growth.

Saving incentiveSupply → right; interest rate ↓; investment ↑

Demand is held fixed.

Investment incentiveDemand → right; interest rate ↑; investment ↑

Supply is held fixed.

Larger budget deficitPublic saving ↓; supply → left; interest rate ↑; investment ↓

This is the supply-of-national-saving representation used in the slides.

Worked example: Explain the chain, not just the arrow

Government purchases rise while taxes and private saving are unchanged. What does the loanable-funds model predict?

  1. Public saving T − G falls.
  2. National saving falls, so the supply of loanable funds shifts left.
  3. The equilibrium interest rate rises.
  4. At the higher interest rate, some private investment is crowded out.

A larger deficit raises the interest rate and reduces private investment, other things equal.

Common mistakes
  • In these slides a deficit shifts the supply curve left; use that representation consistently.
  • Saving and investment incentives both raise investment but move the interest rate in opposite directions.
  • Crowding out refers to reduced private investment, not a claim that all private investment disappears.

Output is 10,000 units, with 200 workers working 10 hours per day. The sheet asks for 'productivity per day'; the choices imply output per worker-hour.

  1. 2
  2. 5
  3. 10
  4. 20

Wording correction: Ambiguous unit: the source asks 'productivity per day' but supplies choices matching productivity per worker-hour. Output's time period is not explicitly stated.

Solution

  1. Total labor input per day = 200 × 10 = 2,000 worker-hours.
  2. If 10,000 is daily output, output per worker-hour = 10,000 ÷ 2,000 = 5.
  3. Daily output per worker would instead be 10,000 ÷ 200 = 50; that value is absent from the choices.

B — 5 units per worker-hour is the likely intended answer; the source's 'per day' wording is inconsistent.

Common mistake: Do not label 5 as units per worker per day. State the unit and the assumption about the output period.

A24. Saving and future output

In the growth model, what does a higher saving rate tend to support?

  1. More consumption today
  2. Less investment
  3. More capital and higher future output
  4. Lower productivity

Solution

  1. More saving makes resources available for investment rather than current consumption.
  2. More capital per worker raises productivity and the future level of output, other things equal.

C — More capital and higher future output.

Common mistake: Diminishing returns mean a higher saving rate does not permanently raise the growth rate.

A25. Compare productivity

Country X produces 20,000 units with 500 workers; Country Y produces 30,000 with 1,000 workers. Which has greater output per worker?

X20,00050040

  1. X
  2. Y
  3. They are equal
  4. Cannot be determined

Solution

  1. X: 20,000 ÷ 500 = 40 units per worker.
  2. Y: 30,000 ÷ 1,000 = 30 units per worker.
  3. 40 > 30, so X is more productive by this measure.

A26. Diminishing returns

What are diminishing returns to capital?

  1. More capital always reduces output
  2. Each additional unit of capital adds less extra output
  3. Productivity always rises at the same rate
  4. Output never changes

Solution

  1. Hold other production inputs and technology fixed.
  2. As capital increases, an additional unit produces a smaller increase in output than the previous unit.

B — Each extra unit of capital adds less output.

Common mistake: Output can still rise; it rises by smaller increments.

A27. Recognize diminishing returns

Capital rises from 10 to 20 and output from 100 to 120. Capital then rises to 30 and output to 130. What pattern is shown?

  1. Increasing returns
  2. Diminishing returns
  3. Constant returns
  4. No returns

Solution

  1. First 10-unit capital increase adds 20 units of output.
  2. Second 10-unit capital increase adds only 10 units of output.
  3. The extra output from equal increments of capital is falling.

B — Diminishing returns to capital.

Common mistake: This is about marginal returns to one input, not returns to scale when all inputs change.

A28. Foreign direct investment

Which benefit of foreign direct investment fits the growth model?

  1. Lower productivity
  2. Technology transfer
  3. Lower wages
  4. Less capital

Solution

  1. FDI brings foreign-owned and operated productive capital.
  2. It can also introduce technology and production knowledge.

B — It can increase technology transfer.

Common mistake: Technology transfer is a potential benefit, not a guarantee for every investment.

A29. Growth per person

Real GDP per person rises from $20,000 to $21,000. Calculate the growth rate.

  1. 1%
  2. 2%
  3. 5%
  4. 10%

Solution

  1. Growth = [(21,000 − 20,000) ÷ 20,000] × 100.
  2. 1,000 ÷ 20,000 × 100 = 5%.

C — 5%.

Which listed policy supports long-run growth in the course model?

  2. Higher barriers to foreign investment

B1. Financial system's role

What is the financial system's main role?

  1. Reduce government spending
  2. Connect savers with borrowers
  3. Control inflation
  4. Increase exports

Solution

  1. Savers provide funds; borrowers use funds for investment.
  2. The financial system brings these sides together.

B — Connect savers with borrowers.

B2. Equity versus debt

A firm sells ownership shares to raise finance. Which type of finance is this?

  1. Debt finance
  2. Equity finance
  3. Retained earnings
  4. Indirect finance

Solution

  1. A share is an ownership claim in the firm.
  2. Raising money by issuing shares is equity finance.

B — Equity finance.

Common mistake: A bond represents debt; a share represents ownership.

B3. Financial intermediary

Which listed institution is a financial intermediary?

  1. A corporate bond
  2. A mutual fund
  3. A Treasury bill
  4. A share of stock

Solution

  1. A mutual fund pools savers' money and invests it in assets.
  2. The other options are financial instruments, not intermediary institutions.

B — A mutual fund.

B4. Saving equals investment

In a closed economy, national saving equals what?

  1. Consumption
  2. Investment
  3. Government purchases
  4. Net exports

Solution

  1. A closed economy has NX = 0, so Y = C + I + G.
  2. Rearrange: Y − C − G = I.
  3. National saving is S = Y − C − G, so S = I.

B — Investment.

Common mistake: S = I here relies on the closed-economy national-income identity.

Source: Review Chapters 26-29-30.docx, question 4

B5. National saving formula

Choose the national saving identity for a closed economy.

  1. S = Y − C
  2. S = Y − T − C
  3. S = Y − C − G
  4. S = T − G

Solution

  1. Add private saving, Y − T − C, and public saving, T − G.
  2. Taxes cancel: S = Y − C − G.

C — S = Y − C − G.

Common mistake: Y − T − C is private saving; T − G is public saving.

Source: Review Chapters 26-29-30.docx, question 5

B6. Public saving formula

Which expression defines public saving?

  1. Y − C − T
  2. T − G
  3. Y − C − G
  4. I − S

Solution

  1. Public saving is tax revenue left after government purchases.
  2. Therefore public saving = T − G.

B — T − G.

Common mistake: The positive size of a budget deficit is G − T; public saving is its negative.

B7. Deficit and public saving

What does a government budget deficit imply?

  1. Public saving is positive
  2. Public saving is zero
  3. Public saving is negative
  4. Private saving must be negative

Solution

  1. A deficit means government purchases exceed taxes: G > T.
  2. Therefore public saving, T − G, is negative.

C — Public saving is negative.

Common mistake: A public deficit alone does not tell you the sign of private saving.

B8. Calculate national saving

Given Y = 800, C = 500 and G = 200, calculate national saving.

  1. 50
  2. 100
  3. 150
  4. 200

Solution

  1. S = Y − C − G.
  2. S = 800 − 500 − 200 = 100.

B — 100.

B9. Calculate private saving

Given Y = 900, C = 600 and T = 150, calculate private saving.

  1. 100
  2. 150
  3. 200
  4. 250

Solution

  1. Private saving = Y − T − C.
  2. 900 − 150 − 600 = 150.

B10. Calculate public saving

Taxes are T = 300 and government purchases are G = 350. Find public saving and the budget position.

  1. +50; surplus
  2. −50; deficit
  3. +50; deficit
  4. −50; surplus

Solution

  1. Public saving = T − G = 300 − 350 = −50.
  2. Government spending exceeds taxes by 50, so the deficit's size is 50.

B — Public saving is −50; the government has a deficit of 50.

B11. Positive national saving

If national saving is positive in a closed economy, which statement follows?

  1. Y − C − G > 0
  2. Investment is positive
  3. At least one of private or public saving is positive
  4. All of these

Solution

  1. S > 0 directly implies Y − C − G > 0.
  2. Since S = I in a closed economy, I > 0.
  3. Since S is private plus public saving, they cannot both be zero or negative if their sum is positive.

D — All three statements follow.

Common mistake: Positive national saving does not require both private and public saving to be positive.

Source: Review Chapters 26-29-30.docx, question 11

B12. Deficit and loanable funds

Holding other factors fixed, what does a larger government deficit do in the course's loanable-funds model?

  1. Increases the supply of loanable funds
  2. Decreases the supply of loanable funds
  3. Decreases demand for loanable funds
  4. Leaves the market unchanged

Solution

  1. A larger deficit lowers public saving.
  2. With private saving unchanged, national saving falls.
  3. The supply curve shifts left; the equilibrium interest rate rises and investment falls.

B — The supply of loanable funds decreases.

Common mistake: This course represents the deficit as a leftward supply shift; distinguish a curve shift from a movement along it.
